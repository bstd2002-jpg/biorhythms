About the menstrual cycle

Summary of Reed BG, Carr BR. The Normal Menstrual Cycle and the Control of Ovulation. Endotext, NCBI Bookshelf (NBK279054), last updated August 5, 2018. Citations give the PDF page (from the page footer), section, and paragraph.

Overall structure

The cycle has two phases, follicular (proliferative) and luteal (secretory), and its length runs from the first day of bleeding to the first day of the next period. The median cycle is 28 days, with most between 25 and 30. [p. 1, Abstract]

The luteal phase is fairly constant at about 14 days; differences in cycle length come mostly from the follicular phase, which ranges from 10 to 16 days. [p. 1, Abstract]

Follicular phase

Runs from the first day of menses until ovulation, with basal body temperature in its lower range. [p. 2, The Follicular Phase, ¶1]

As the previous cycle's corpus luteum winds down and inhibin A falls, FSH rises in the last days of the cycle and recruits a group of follicles. After the period starts, FSH declines under negative feedback from estrogen and inhibin B. [p. 2, The Follicular Phase, ¶2]

The dominant follicle develops in stages: recruitment on days 1–4, selection on days 5–7, and dominance by about day 8. [p. 3, ¶1 after Figure 2]

Estradiol rises as the follicle grows. [p. 3, ¶1 after Figure 3] LH starts low and begins rising by mid-follicular phase through positive feedback from rising estrogen. [p. 5, last ¶]

Ovulation

A sharp rise in estradiol from the preovulatory follicle triggers the LH surge. The surge begins about 34–36 hours before ovulation, and ovulation follows the LH peak by about 10–12 hours. [p. 7, Ovulation, ¶1; Figure 5]

Estradiol drops sharply just before the LH peak, and progesterone drives the midcycle rise in FSH. [p. 9, ¶1 after Figure 7]

Luteal phase

Usually about 14 days. The remaining follicle becomes the corpus luteum, which mainly secretes progesterone to prepare the uterine lining. [p. 9, Luteal Phase, ¶1]

Progesterone and estradiol peak about 8–9 days after ovulation, around the expected time of implantation. [p. 10, ¶1]

Estrogen peaks twice: it rises mid-follicular, falls steeply after ovulation, rises again mid-luteal alongside progesterone, then falls at the end of the cycle. [p. 10, ¶1 after Figure 8]

The corpus luteum starts to decline 9–11 days after ovulation unless pregnancy occurs. [p. 12, ¶1]

Menstruation

Falling progesterone makes the spiral arteries constrict, cutting blood flow to the lining, which is then shed. Within two days of bleeding starting, estrogen from growing follicles begins repairing the lining. [p. 15, Menstruation, ¶1]

Flow lasts four to six days on average, with a normal range of two to eight. [p. 15, Menstruation, ¶2]

Cervical mucus

Scant and thick just after a period; clear, abundant, and stretchy in the late follicular phase as estradiol rises (about 30 times more than early in the cycle); thick, opaque, and scant again after ovulation as progesterone rises. [p. 14–15, Cervix, ¶1]

Body temperature

Low through the follicular phase, then a jump of roughly 0.3–0.5 °C after ovulation that holds through the luteal phase. [p. 1, Figure 1, body temperature panel] How this shows up across the day is covered under "About daily rhythms".

              Two processes control sleep

              When you sleep is set by two interacting processes: a sleep drive (Process S) that builds the longer you're awake and clears during sleep, and a circadian rhythm (Process C) from the brain's clock that promotes waking by day and sleep by night. [StatPearls REM, p. 2; Medscape, p. 2]

              The clock's alerting signal rises through the day and peaks about 2–3 hours before your usual bedtime, holding off the sleep pressure built up during the day. In the evening, rising melatonin quiets it, letting sleep begin. [Doghramji, p. S17–S18, Fig. 1]

              The body clock and melatonin

              The clock sits in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus. Its natural cycle is a little longer than 24 hours (about 24.2) and is reset each day mainly by light, with exercise, social activity, and mealtimes as weaker cues. [Medscape, p. 3; Doghramji, p. S17]

              Melatonin is made by the pineal gland in darkness and switched off by light. It rises after dark, peaks in the middle of the sleep period (about 2–4 AM), and falls gradually during the second half of the night. [Doghramji, p. S18]

              Light's effect on the clock depends on timing: light in the evening and first half of the night moves it later, light late at night and in the early morning moves it earlier. [Tähkämö, p. 153] Evening and night light delay it reliably: 1 hour at night delayed melatonin by about 36 minutes, and even red light, which barely suppresses melatonin, delayed it by almost an hour. [Tähkämö, p. 163–164] Morning light is less dependable on its own: 1 hour of blue light after waking did no better than dim light, but morning light combined with an earlier sleep schedule or blocked evening light moved the clock 1–2 hours earlier over 1–2 weeks. [Tähkämö, p. 162–165] Brief flashes and light through closed eyelids can also shift the clock. [Tähkämö, p. 164, 166–167]

              Light lowers melatonin within minutes; after a 30-minute pulse it was lowest 30–45 minutes after the light came on and recovered within about 15–30 minutes of it ending. [Tähkämö, p. 161, 165]

              Melatonin taken as a supplement shifts the clock in roughly the opposite direction to light: evening doses move it earlier, morning doses later. [Doghramji, p. S18]

              Cortisol

              Cortisol, the main stress hormone, follows a strong daily rhythm: highest around 8–9 AM and lowest around 10 PM–midnight. [Kulzhanova, p. 8171; Baker, p. 616] On top of that comes the cortisol awakening response: a rise of 38–75% within about 30 minutes of waking, highest 30–60 minutes after. [Ozgocer, p. 58, 60] Afternoon levels are fairly flat. [Montero-López, p. 69]

              How high cortisol runs is largely a personal trait: across a whole cycle each woman's morning burst stayed within her own range, but ranges differed 10- to 100-fold between women. Brighter morning light and waking earlier make the burst larger, and it is smaller on weekends. [Ozgocer, p. 61–62, 64, 66] Exercise raises cortisol only above about half of maximum effort for about 20 minutes, peaking 20–30 minutes after; a 10-minute all-out test didn't. [Prado, p. 8]

              Across the menstrual cycle the daily pattern looks the same in both phases. [Montero-López, p. 70–71] Overall levels are slightly higher in the follicular than the luteal phase: a small effect in a meta-analysis of 28 studies (Hedges' g = 0.13), clearest in morning and blood samples, and 11.0 vs 8.6 µg/dL in one study. [Kulzhanova, p. 8173, 8176; Prado, p. 7] A proposed reason is that a progesterone metabolite (allopregnanolone) calms the brain's stress-hormone center in the luteal phase. [Kulzhanova, p. 8175; Prado, p. 8] The luteal daily swing may also be smaller. [Baker, p. 616] But results conflict: in daily tracking, the morning burst rose with each woman's estradiol and progesterone [Ozgocer, p. 61, 64], and the cortisol response to a stressful task was larger in the luteal phase in one study. [Montero-López, p. 70–71]

              Sleep pressure and adenosine

              Adenosine, a by-product of brain cells using energy, builds up during waking and acts on A1 and A2A receptors to quiet wake-promoting brain areas and deepen sleep. [Reichert, p. 3–4]

              Measured adenosine changes within minutes, not hours: it jumps when you wake, holds steady, and drops at sleep onset. So on its own it probably doesn't explain the slow build-up of sleepiness over a normal day, but it rises further when you stay awake much longer than usual. [Reichert, p. 4]

              After sleep loss, A1 receptors become more plentiful in the human brain and return to normal only after recovery sleep. Sleep debt leads to longer, deeper sleep the next night. [Reichert, p. 13; Doghramji, p. S18]

              Caffeine blocks A1 and A2A receptors. About 200 mg in the early evening delayed the melatonin rhythm by roughly 40 minutes. [Reichert, p. 2, p. 4]

              Sleep stages

              Sleep has REM and three non-REM stages: N1 (lightest, about 2–5% of sleep), N2 (about 45–55%), and N3 (deep, slow-wave sleep, about 10–25%). REM, the dreaming stage in which the muscles are slack, makes up about 20–25%. [StatPearls Stages, p. 3–4; Medscape, p. 1]

              Stages run in cycles, usually N1 → N2 → N3 → N2 → REM, 4–5 times a night. Cycles last about 70–100 minutes early in the night and 90–120 minutes later. [StatPearls Stages, p. 3; Medscape, p. 1]

              Deep sleep is concentrated early and REM late. In healthy young adults, N3 was about half of the first cycle and under a tenth of the fourth, while REM rose from about 10% to 25%. The first REM period is short (under 10 minutes) and the last can exceed an hour. [Shirota, Fig. 1, p. 6; Medscape, p. 1]

              Brief awakenings are normal, about a minute on average. Sleep almost always resumes through light sleep: after waking, the next stage was N1 about 70% of the time and deep N3 about 1%. Waking naturally in the morning usually comes out of REM. [Shirota, Tables 2–3, p. 5–6; StatPearls Stages, p. 5]

              The first night somewhere new is lighter and more broken, mostly in the first cycle, and settles by the second night. [Shirota, Table 1 and Fig. 1, p. 5–6]

              Substances and sleep stages

              Nearly all antidepressants, including SSRIs such as sertraline, delay the first REM period and reduce total REM. Alcohol also delays the first REM period and reduces REM. [StatPearls REM, p. 4–5; StatPearls Stages, p. 7; Medscape, p. 2]

              Cannabis at the doses studied in recent trials (about 2.5–15 mg THC) showed no consistent effect on any sleep stage. Older studies with high doses (70–210 mg THC) reduced REM; the lowest dose with a clear REM reduction was 20 mg THC, and the effect faded with repeated nightly use as tolerance built. [Velzeboer, p. 9–12; Practice Points, p. 13]

              Stopping after regular use is different: in the first days of withdrawal people slept less, took longer to fall asleep, slept less efficiently, and had more REM that started earlier, often with vivid dreams. [Velzeboer, p. 9–13] Most studies were small, mostly used oral THC, and had a high risk of bias, so these findings are uncertain. [Velzeboer, p. 13]

              Meal timing and the eating window

              Light sets the master clock in the brain, but the clocks in the liver, gut, pancreas, fat tissue and muscle are also set by when you eat, so food (and stopping eating) is one of the main cues that keeps them on a 24-hour rhythm. [Currenti, p. 726–727; Jaffe, p. 344] Eating spread over a long part of the day, or at irregular times, can disrupt these rhythms, and long-term disruption is linked to obesity, high blood pressure, insulin resistance and unhealthy blood fats. [Wilkinson, p. 92–93] Late-night eating, made easy by artificial light, is one form of this misalignment and is linked to weight gain. [Currenti, p. 727; Jaffe, p. 344] Long windows are common: in app-based tracking, over half of people ate across more than 15 hours a day. [Wilkinson, p. 93]

              Heart and metabolism. In a 12-week study of 19 adults with metabolic syndrome who usually ate across 14 hours or more, eating within a self-chosen 10-hour window (most chose about 8–10 AM to 6–8 PM) lowered weight by about 3%, systolic and diastolic blood pressure by about 5 and 6.5 mmHg, and LDL cholesterol by 11%, on top of statins and blood pressure drugs, with no reported side effects. People also reported waking rested on more days (70% → 88%). [Wilkinson, p. 93–94, 99, Table 1 p. 97] It was a small study with no control group. [Wilkinson, p. 102]

              Mental health. Fasting for several hours raises BDNF, a growth factor for brain cells that is low in people with depression and insomnia, and one study found early time-restricted eating raised BDNF in people. [Currenti, p. 725–726, 729] Meal timing may also affect mood through sleep: eating is a cue for the body clock, and the clock and sleep in turn affect depression. [Jaffe, p. 344–345] Evidence in people is early. One randomized trial found lower depression scores with time-restricted eating than with a control diet, while uncontrolled studies found no change. In a survey of 207 people with bipolar disorder who tried eating within under 14 hours, about half reported improvements in mood. [Johnson, p. 3] No trial has yet tested time-restricted eating as a depression treatment. [Jaffe, p. 345] Benefits seem larger when the window is earlier in the day. [Currenti, p. 729; Jaffe, p. 344]

              Cautions. Limiting when you eat isn't suitable for everyone: people with an eating disorder or a history of one, people at risk of malnutrition, and people on insulin (who risk low blood sugar) should not start without their clinician. The word "restricted" can encourage rigid eating, so it's better treated as part of a regular daily routine with sleep and exercise than as a diet. [Currenti, p. 729; Jaffe, p. 345; Johnson, p. 1] The 10-hour flag here is information, not a target.

              The menstrual cycle and daily rhythms

              In healthy women the body clock keeps the same timing through the cycle: the daily temperature low and the start, end, and midpoint of melatonin don't move between the follicular and luteal phases. [Shechter, p. 651, Table 2 p. 653; Baker, p. 615–616] Whether the total amount of melatonin changes is unclear: older studies conflict, and one controlled study found less across 24 hours in the luteal phase. [Baker, p. 615–616; Shechter, p. 651]

              Body temperature normally swings 0.8–1 °C between a daytime high and a nighttime low. [Baker, p. 614] In the luteal phase progesterone raises it by about 0.4 °C, about a day after progesterone rises, and blunts the nighttime dip so the daily swing is smaller (0.31 vs 0.23 °C in one study). Estrogen lowers temperature, so the 24-hour average is lowest just before ovulation. [Baker, p. 615; Shechter, p. 651, Table 2 p. 653] Cortisol and thyroid-stimulating hormone also swing less, suggesting the clock's output is slightly weaker, not shifted, in the luteal phase. [Baker, p. 616]

              Deep sleep, sleep efficiency, and waking during the night stay the same across the cycle, so the sleep-pressure side is unaffected. REM is somewhat lower in the luteal phase (25.2% vs 22.7% of the night in one study, mostly in the middle of the night), probably through progesterone's effects on GABAA receptors. In the same study, falling asleep took longer (16 vs 7 minutes) and women felt groggier on waking in the luteal phase. Sleep spindles (bursts of activity in N2) increase in the luteal phase. [Baker, p. 616; Shechter, Table 1 p. 650, p. 651, 653, 655]

              Sleep feels worst in the days before a period and the first few days of it, even though measured sleep changes little. [Baker, p. 616]

              Hormonal birth control replaces the natural cycle: on the pill, body temperature is raised much as in the luteal phase, nighttime melatonin tends to be higher, and there is less deep sleep. These effects are larger than the natural cycle's. [Baker, p. 618, Table 1 p. 617]

              Methods: how the estimates are made

              Every curve and prediction on this page is a model estimate built from what you log, using timings from the sources in the two "About" sections. Many of the numbers below are placeholders chosen to match those sources' descriptions; they are not fitted to your data. This is not medical advice and is not reliable enough for contraception or diagnosis.

              1. Cycles, periods, and predictions

              • Logged period days no more than 2 days apart count as one period. A cycle starts on the first day of each period, and a cycle's length is the number of days to the next start.
              • Average cycle length: the mean of completed cycles, or 28 days if there are none yet.
              • Average period length: first to last logged day of each period that has ended (more than 2 days since its last logged day), averaged; 5 days if there are none yet.
              • Next period start: the current cycle's start plus its expected length. If ovulation has been estimated from discharge this cycle, the expected length is ovulation day plus your luteal length; otherwise it's your average cycle length. The predicted period lasts your average period length (hollow dots on the calendar).

              2. Ovulation and cycle phases

              • From discharge: ovulation is placed on the last day in a cycle with watery or egg-white discharge, ignoring period days, because clear, stretchy mucus appears just before ovulation and turns thick after it (Reed & Carr, p. 14–15). In completed cycles it must fall at least 7 days before the next period.
              • In the current cycle, that day is tentative until 3 days pass without another watery or egg-white day.
              • Luteal length (ovulation to next period): the average over completed cycles with a discharge-based ovulation, keeping values of 7–20 days; otherwise 14 days (Reed & Carr, p. 1).
              • Without discharge: ovulation = cycle length − luteal length.
              • Phases: follicular from the first day of the period to the day before ovulation, then the ovulation day, then luteal until the next period.
              • Circle view: one turn is the cycle's length (for several cycles, the longest one shown), with today at the top. Each day is a circle shaded by phase. The period is drawn as a band inside the ring rather than as a phase of its own, because menstruation is the first part of the follicular phase (Reed & Carr, p. 1). Hormones are drawn outside the ring, reaching further out the higher they are. The › arrow (or scrolling or swiping sideways) past the current cycle shows the predicted next cycle: it starts on the predicted period date, has your average cycle length, a period of your average length, and ovulation one average luteal phase before its end. Hormonal birth control: the charts show estimates for a combined pill instead (21 active days, then 7 pill-free days placed around your logged bleed: the last 2 days of the cycle and the first 5; the withdrawal bleed happens in the pill-free week, Baerwald & Pierson, p. 2). On active pills the pill's estrogen and progestin suppress FSH and LH, and the progestin blocks the LH surge (p. 1), so FSH is held at about a third of its early-follicular level, LH and estradiol near their floor, and progesterone low (the pill's progestin is not progesterone). Suppression is incomplete, though, and is lost in the pill-free week (p. 1, 3): dominant follicles of 10 mm or more appear in up to 86% of pill users then, and by its end FSH and estradiol reach early-follicular levels (p. 5). So FSH rises linearly back to its early-follicular level (0.10 → 0.28), LH slightly, and estradiol late (0.05 → 0.10). Restarting pills lowers FSH (p. 5): it falls back with a 2-day time constant, so about a week of active pills re-suppresses it (Birtch 2005, §1.3.4). Estradiol levels off for about a day before falling, because a follicle that has started growing can keep growing as FSH declines (p. 5). Not modelled: pill strength (20 µg ethinyl estradiol pills allow more FSH, LH and follicle growth than 30–35 µg ones, p. 4); a later start ("Sunday start"), missed pills or a longer pill-free week, which all raise the chance of follicle growth and ovulation (p. 4–6); shorter or no pill-free weeks, which suppress more (p. 5); and the occasional follicle that reaches pre-ovulatory size with pre-ovulatory estradiol (p. 3). Sizes are placeholders on the natural-cycle scale. Sources: Baerwald AR, Pierson RA. Ovarian follicular development during the use of oral contraception: a review. J Obstet Gynaecol Can 2004;26(1):19–24 (author manuscript pages 1–6); Birtch RL. Image analysis of dominant ovarian follicles and ovarian follicular development during continuous and conventional oral contraceptive dosing schemes (MSc thesis, University of Saskatchewan, 2005); Pralong F. Oral contraception and the HPG axis (Geneva Foundation for Medical Education and Research, 2005). Cycle history shows only finished cycles, as rings (oldest in the middle) or as bars like the sleep patterns. The optional fertile window is the 5 days before the estimated ovulation day plus that day, the usual convention based on how long sperm and the egg survive; it inherits all the uncertainty of the ovulation estimate and is not suitable for contraception. The optional premenstrual week is the last 7 days of the cycle, when sleep often feels worse (Baker, p. 616). Averages for several cycles: cycle and luteal length use finished cycles only; period length uses periods that are over; ovulation day uses every cycle shown.

              3. Hormone curves (menstrual cycle)

              • Each hormone is drawn as smooth peaks placed relative to the ovulation day (ov, counted in days from the first day of the period). Each peak can rise and fall at different rates. Each hormone has its own 0–1 scale, so heights show shape, not concentrations, and can't be compared between hormones.
              • LH: a sharp peak 0.45 days (~11 h) before ovulation, starting ~1.4 days (~34 h) before (Reed & Carr, p. 7).
              • FSH: highest around the first day of the period and rising again at the end of the cycle, plus a smaller surge alongside LH (p. 2, p. 7).
              • Estradiol: a main peak 1.5 days before ovulation that falls sharply, and a second peak 8 days after ovulation at about two-thirds the height (p. 5, p. 10; Table 1).
              • Progesterone: low until ovulation, peaking 8.5 days after and back to baseline by the next period (p. 9–10, p. 12).

              4. Body clock

              • The clock is tracked as the time of the melatonin peak, the middle of the sleep schedule it's set to. Your usual sleep length is the average of logged nights over the past 7 days (8 hours if none).
              • It's simulated day by day, starting 21 days before the dates shown, from your average logged mid-sleep (3 AM if none).
              • Sleep: each day it moves half of the way toward that night's logged mid-sleep, but by at most 1 hour later or 30 minutes earlier (shifting earlier is harder).
              • Nights you don't log: it moves a quarter of the way back toward your usual mid-sleep (the average of logged nights in the 14 days before; 3 AM if none), with the same limits. This stands in for the everyday light and routine that keep a real clock from drifting, so a shift from one day's light fades instead of lasting forever.
              • Light: the clock's most light-sensitive point is set ~2 hours before your usual wake time. Light before it moves the clock later and light after it moves it earlier, most strongly ~3.5 hours either side and fading further away (Tähkämö, p. 153). One hour of bright light at the most sensitive time moves it up to 30 minutes later (1 hour of light at night delayed it ~36 minutes: Tähkämö, p. 164) or 15 minutes earlier. Advances are half as large because morning light alone shifted the clock little in real-world studies unless sleep was also moved earlier or evening light was blocked (Tähkämö, p. 162–163, 165). Exposures longer than 2 hours count as 2. Indoor light counts 0.3× and screens 0.15×.
              • Melatonin supplement: shifts the clock in the opposite direction to light (evening: earlier, morning: later), up to 0.5 hours for ~3 mg at the most effective time, ~7 hours from the sensitive point (Doghramji, p. S18).
              • Caffeine: in the 6 hours before your usual bedtime, delays the clock 40 minutes per 200 mg, up to 1 hour (Reichert, p. 2).
              • All of a day's cues together move the clock at most 1.5 hours later or 1 hour earlier.
              • Not modelled: the clock's natural drift later (~24.2-hour cycle) without cues (the pull toward your usual timing replaces it), light color (blue vs red), flashing light, light through closed eyes, and meals or exercise as cues. Both are logged or described but don't affect the clock yet.

              5. Daily rhythm curves

              • Nights you don't log use the body clock's usual bedtime and wake time. Logged awakenings are removed from sleep.
              • Melatonin: a peak at the clock's mid-sleep, rising from ~2 hours before usual bedtime and falling more gradually until ~1.5 hours after usual wake. Logged light cuts its production (bright 70%, indoor 30%, screens 15%); the level follows with a 10-minute half-time, so it falls over about 30 minutes and recovers within about 30 minutes after the light ends (Tähkämö, p. 161, 165). A supplement adds a short peak (absorbed with a 15-minute half-time and cleared with a 45-minute half-life; Doghramji gives 0.5–5.6 minutes, most other sources 20–60).
              • Alerting signal (Process C): lowest (0.1) 1 hour before usual wake, highest (0.9) 2.5 hours before usual bedtime, changing smoothly between (Doghramji, Fig. 1).
              • Sleep pressure (Process S): while awake it rises toward 1 with an 18.2-hour time constant; while asleep it falls toward 0 with a 4.2-hour time constant (the classic two-process-model values). Calculated in 6-minute steps from 2 days earlier so it has settled.
              • Adenosine: moves toward 0.55 while awake and 0.15 while asleep within about 10 minutes. After 16 hours awake it climbs up to 0.35 higher over the next 8 hours (Reichert, p. 4, p. 13).
              • Sleep propensity: 0.5 + sleep pressure − alerting signal, kept between 0 and 1.1.
              • Body temperature (right-hand axis, °C): lowest 3.5 hours before usual wake time (Shechter, Table 2), highest 14 hours after that, changing smoothly between. The follicular daily average is 36.8 °C with a ±0.45 °C swing (0.9 °C peak to trough; Baker, p. 614). See section 8 for cycle effects.
              • Cortisol (relative, no units, since personal levels differ up to 100-fold): a smooth daily curve through 0.08 at 30 minutes before usual bedtime, 0.15 at mid-sleep, 0.55 at usual wake, 0.45 four hours later, and 0.3 nine hours after waking (Kulzhanova, p. 8171; Montero-López, p. 69). On top of that:
                • an awakening burst of +0.3 (about 55%) after each night's actual wake time, highest 35 minutes after waking and fading over about 1.5 hours (Ozgocer, p. 58, 60);
                • logged light starting from 15 minutes before to 1 hour after waking makes that burst up to 20% larger (bright 20%, indoor 6%, screens 3%; direction from Ozgocer, p. 64, size a placeholder);
                • exercise of 20 minutes or more adds a bump peaking 25 minutes after it ends: +0.12 moderate, +0.22 vigorous; light exercise or shorter sessions add nothing (Prado, p. 8).
              • Solid lines are up to the current time; dashed lines are predictions.

              6. Sleep stages

              • Each night is split into sleep bouts at logged awakenings (shown as Awake).
              • Cycles last 85, 95, 100, and 105 minutes, then 110; numbering continues across awakenings.
              • Each bout starts with 5 minutes of N1. Each cycle then runs N2 (60% of its N2) → N3 → N2 (40%) → REM.
              • REM per cycle: 10, 19, 22, and 26 minutes, then 6 minutes more each cycle, up to 60 (Shirota, Fig. 1; Medscape, p. 1).
              • N3 per cycle: 42 minutes × (sleep pressure at the cycle's start − 0.12) ÷ (0.6 − 0.12), between 0 and 1.5×, so deep sleep is largest early and after sleep loss. Every cycle keeps at least 10 minutes of N2.
              • Sertraline within 36 hours before sleep: REM × 0.7 and none in the first cycle. Alcohol within 6 hours before sleep: REM × 0.85 and none in the first cycle (StatPearls REM, p. 4–5; StatPearls Stages, p. 7).
              • Cannabis (logged in mg THC; doses within 6 hours before sleep are added up). Below 20 mg: no change. At 20 mg or more: REM reduced by 25%, rising linearly to 45% at 70 mg or more, and no REM in the first cycle. Each consecutive earlier night at 20 mg or more keeps 70% of the effect (tolerance) (Velzeboer, p. 11–12).
              • Cannabis withdrawal: if THC was logged on at least 5 of the 7 days up to the last dose, nights from 1 to 7 days after it get up to 40% more REM and a first cycle up to 25 minutes shorter (an earlier first REM), both fading linearly to nothing by day 7. The chart note also flags that falling asleep may take longer and sleep may be shorter; the model doesn't change your logged sleep times (Velzeboer, p. 9–12). All of these sizes are placeholders: the review reports directions and pooled effect sizes, not minutes per night.
              • Not modelled: the first-night effect, brief arousals you didn't log, age and sex differences, and the natural night-to-night variation in stage timing.

              7. Sleep patterns

              • 24-hour clock view (Daily rhythms): the same curves for the selected day, midnight to midnight, with midnight at the top, laid out like the cycle circle: an hour ring in the middle (shaded where you were asleep), each curve as a shaded area outside it that reaches further out the higher it is (each one scaled from its own lowest to highest point that day, with a minimum range of 0.3 so flat curves stay flat; hover for the actual values), and inside it the estimated sleep stages (drawn thicker for deeper stages: Awake thinnest, N3 thickest) and logged events.
              • Each bar runs from logged bedtime to wake time for the night ending on that date. Averages and "varies ±" (the standard deviation, in minutes) use only logged nights in the chosen window.
              • Eating window: for each date, the time from the first to the last logged meal or snack, the same "daily eating interval" Wilkinson used (Wilkinson, p. 94). Entries before 4 AM count toward the previous date (as times past midnight), so a late snack lengthens that day instead of starting the next one. Days with a single entry show as a dot and are left out of the averages. A day, or the average, is flagged when the window is over 10 hours, the window tested in Wilkinson. Only what you log is counted, so a missed snack makes the window look shorter.

              8. Menstrual cycle effects on daily rhythms

              • Each moment on the daily rhythms chart is placed in your logged or predicted cycle (section 1–2); past the expected end of the current cycle, cycles repeat with the same length. Progesterone and the pre-ovulatory estradiol peak come from the hormone curves (section 3), scaled 0–1.
              • Timing: melatonin, the alerting signal, and sleep propensity are not changed, because controlled studies find no shift in clock timing (Shechter, p. 651; Baker, p. 615–616).
              • Temperature: daily average + 0.4 °C × progesterone from 1 day earlier (Baker, p. 615); − 0.1 °C × the pre-ovulatory estradiol peak (direction from Baker, p. 615; size is a placeholder); daily swing × (1 − 0.26 × progesterone), from 0.31 → 0.23 °C (Shechter, Table 2).
              • REM: each night's REM × (1 − 0.1 × progesterone at bedtime), from 25.2% → 22.7% of the night (Shechter, Table 1). Applied evenly through the night, though the study found the drop mostly in its middle third.
              • Cortisol: daily swing around 0.3 × (1 − 0.15 × progesterone), then the whole curve × (1 − 0.1 × progesterone), so it is up to 10% lower and swings 15% less at the luteal peak. The evidence is small and conflicting (Kulzhanova, p. 8173; Prado, p. 7; Baker, p. 616; against: Ozgocer, p. 61, 64; Montero-López, p. 70–71), so the sizes are deliberately modest placeholders. No change with hormonal birth control, whose effects on cortisol are mixed (Kulzhanova, p. 8176; Montero-López, p. 68).
              • Notes: the chart note mentions the luteal phase when progesterone is above half its peak, period days 1–4, and the 7 days before the next expected period (Baker, p. 616; Shechter, p. 650–651).
              • Combined pill (birth control setting in Settings): no REM change or cycle notes; temperature is held at the luteal level (+0.4 °C, smaller swing), as the pill gives a luteal-like temperature rhythm (Baker, p. 618, Table 1). The pill's effects on melatonin and deep sleep are not modelled.
              • Not modelled: the longer time to fall asleep in the luteal phase (your logged bedtime is taken as sleep onset), sleep spindles, and premenstrual dysphoric disorder, where luteal melatonin is later and lower (Baker, p. 619).
